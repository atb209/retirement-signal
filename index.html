The idea

The signal in three steps

  1. Own the market

    Normally the money sits in an S&P 500 fund, a Nasdaq-100 fund, or a mix of the two: hundreds of the largest US companies for a tiny yearly fee. The four options differ in which.

  2. Check two warning signs, once a month

    Is the stock market's trend pointing down? Has the bond market flashed its classic recession warning? Most months at least one answer is no, and nothing happens.

  3. Step aside, then get back in

    When both signs are on, each option sells its set amount (all of its stock funds, part or half) and parks that money in US government bonds. When prices recover, it buys back.

With the signal US stocks, never selling Signal said step aside

The wide band is the 34 months the signal sat out after October 1929. Point at the chart to read any month. It uses a log scale, like the chart at the top.

What it caught: 1929

Both warning signs were on at the end of October 1929, after the first leg of the crash. The signal sold, 23% below the peak, and sat in T-bills for 34 months. Stocks went on to lose 83%.

What it missed: 1937

Stocks fell 50% in 1937–38 and the signal never sold. Short-term interest rates were under 1% for nearly all of 1932 to 1947, so the yield curve could not invert and the recession warning stayed off. Rates were pinned that low again after 2008 and in 2020–2022.

How close 1929 was

The 24-month warning ran out in January 1930, three months after the sale. That spring, stocks came within 2% of the level that triggers a buy-back. Had they crossed it, the signal would have bought back in and held through the rest of the fall.

How this test differs from the main one. Up to 1953 the interest rates are older records: long-term government bond yields minus short-term government rates, collected by the National Bureau of Economic Research and published on FRED. Stock prices come from a long daily record of US stocks, which follows the S&P 500's predecessor from 1928. While stepped aside, all the money sits in T-bills. Trading a month late still averaged – a year. With only a handful of sales in 48 years, read this as supporting evidence, not proof.

How these numbers were made. Month-end prices with dividends reinvested. S&P 500 history from a public daily dataset; Nasdaq-100 and recent prices from FRED (Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis). Treasury returns rebuilt from the 10-year yield; T-bill returns from 3-month rates. Nasdaq-100 dividends estimated at 0.7% a year. A 0.1% trading cost on every trade. No taxes: the plan is designed for a retirement account such as an IRA. The 1920–1967 test uses older records, described in its own section.
