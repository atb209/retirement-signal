How it works

Two warning signs, one check a month

The signal doesn't try to predict the market. It says stay invested until two independent warning signs agree, then step aside until prices recover. Here is what each piece means.

The options

Four ways to use the signal

Every option holds low-cost index funds and follows the same signal on the same dates. They differ in which funds they hold and in how much they sell when the signal says to step aside.

An S&P 500 fund

Owns the 500 largest US companies in one purchase: Apple, Microsoft, Amazon, banks, retailers, healthcare and more.

A Nasdaq-100 fund

Owns the 100 largest companies on the Nasdaq exchange, mostly technology. It has grown faster than the S&P 500, with bigger swings.

Treasuries and T-bills

Loans to the US government, which pays interest. Where money waits safely after a sell signal.

Warning sign 1

The stock market's trend turns down

At the end of every month, take the S&P 500's closing level for each of the last 10 months and average them. That average moves slowly, so it shows the direction prices have been heading.

When the month closes below that average, prices have been falling long enough to matter. That is a broken trend.

On its own, this sign gives too many false alarms: markets dip below their average fairly often and then bounce right back. So it needs a second witness.

Warning sign 2

The bond market flashes a recession warning

The US government borrows money for different lengths of time, from a few months to 30 years. Normally it pays a higher interest rate to borrow for longer, the way a 5-year CD usually pays more than a 3-month one.

Sometimes that flips: short-term rates rise above long-term rates. This is called an inverted yield curve. It usually means investors expect the economy to weaken and interest rates to fall.

The signal compares the 10-year Treasury rate with the 3-month T-bill rate. The first month the 3-month rate is higher, the recession warning turns on, and it stays on for 24 months from that start, however long the rates stay flipped. A new 24-month clock starts only if rates go back to normal for at least six months and then flip again.

On these monthly numbers the curve inverted before every recession since 1968 except 1990, and each of those recessions began 6 to 17 months after the inversion started. That is why the warning runs for 24 months from the start. It isn't perfect, either: it has inverted without a recession following quickly, which is why it never triggers a sale on its own.

The rules

Putting it together

At each month-end there are only three possible outcomes. Any trade happens on the next trading day, then nothing changes until the next month-end.

Most months

Stay invested

If either warning sign is off, do nothing. Every option stays fully invested.

Both signs on

Step aside

The S&P 500 closes below its 10-month average and the recession warning is on: each option sells its set amount: all of it, part of it or half. That money goes half into Treasuries, half into T-bills.

While stepped aside

Get back in when the trend recovers

As soon as the S&P 500 closes a month back above its 10-month average, buy back to fully invested, even if the news still looks bad.

    The reasoning

    Why it's built this way

    Why wait for prices to confirm

    Signals that tried to sell before prices turned (high valuations, rising unemployment, the yield curve alone) lost money or at best broke even in testing. Waiting for the trend to break was the key ingredient.

    Why the Nasdaq-100 is sold completely

    Nothing caught the 2000 tech peak, but the trend break got out early in the crash, before most of the Nasdaq’s fall. In testing, a 100% Nasdaq-100 option that sold only half had a worst drop of 58%, against 34% when it sold everything.

    All out or partly out

    Options that sell everything grew faster in testing, because they avoided more of the deep bear markets. But each false alarm cost them about twice as much as an option that keeps some stocks. That is the main trade-off between the options.

    Why Treasuries and T-bills

    Treasuries usually rise in value during recessions, adding to the protection. T-bills cover the opposite case, when interest rates jump, as in 2022.

    Why 10 months and 24 months

    The exact numbers aren't magic. Averages from 8 to 12 months gave similar results. A warning clock anywhere from 20 to 30 months caught every major decline since 1968; shorter clocks missed one.

    Why only once a month

    Checking more often caused more false alarms and more trades, without better results. Mid-month swings are ignored on purpose.

    The limits

    What it won't do

    • Protect against every drop. Fast crashes and bear markets without a recession get little or no protection: 1987, 2020 and 2022 among them.
    • Avoid small losses. At least half the sales are false alarms that end with buying back a little higher. That is the price of being out for the big ones.
    • Beat the market in calm years. From 2010 to 2026 the signal produced only false alarms, and every option trailed simply holding its own funds.
    • Promise the future. Most of the benefit comes from about six recession bear markets since 1968. That is a small sample, and the future may not look like the past.
